# Fertilizer from Anaerobically Digested Cyanobacterial Biomass

**Evidence label:** Established controlled laboratory evidence; a closed Mars fertilizer-production system remains plausible extrapolation
**Last checked:** 2026-10-30
**Author:** Habitat Sol research scan
**Scope:** What a 2026 laboratory study establishes about converting cyanobacterial biomass into a hydroponic nutrient source, including tests with Mars Global Simulant MGS-1.

## Short answer

A controlled Earth laboratory study showed that anaerobic digestion can convert cyanobacterial biomass into an ammonium-bearing digestate that supported growth of *Lemna* sp. The best tested MGS-1 contribution was a trace-element-supplemented leachate, and crop yield remained about half that obtained on the study's standard medium; the experiment does not demonstrate a closed, food-safe, reliable Mars agriculture loop.

## What we know

- **Anaerobic digestion converted cyanobacterial biomass into a candidate hydroponic fertilizer stream.**
  - **Evidence:** Ramalho et al. varied biomass pre-treatment (none, sonication, or autoclaving), digestion temperature (25, 30, 35, or 45 °C), and substrate concentration (1,250–9,000 mg chemical oxygen demand per litre). Autoclaving and operation at 35 °C produced the greatest organic-carbon removal among the tested conditions. [1]
  - **Conditions / limits:** These are comparative laboratory optima within the reported test matrix, not a full process design. The paper does not establish long-duration stability, complete mass closure, energy demand, or performance in Martian gravity and radiation.

- **Ammonium output tracked biomass input across the tested concentration series.**
  - **Evidence:** The study reports a linear correlation between cyanobacterial biomass input and ammonium output over its substrate-concentration tests. [1]
  - **Conditions / limits:** A correlation inside this controlled range does not establish indefinite scalability, nutrient balance for other crops, or safe operating limits for ammonium accumulation.

- **MGS-1 did not eliminate the need for added trace elements.**
  - **Evidence:** The researchers tested MGS-1 as direct additions of 10, 20, 50, and 200 g/L and as a 10 g/L leachate, with and without trace-element supplementation. The trace-element-supplemented leachate produced the highest reported recovery of phosphate, ammonium, and methane. [1]
  - **Conditions / limits:** MGS-1 is a simulant, not returned Martian material. The successful condition still required trace-element supplementation, and methane is a process coproduct that requires containment and management rather than an automatic system benefit.

- **The digestate supported plant growth, but did not match the standard growth medium.**
  - **Evidence:** *Lemna* sp., a candidate space crop, produced 27 g wet mass per gram of cyanobacterial dry mass supplied through the tested process—approximately half the yield obtained with the study's standard medium. [1]
  - **Conditions / limits:** Wet mass is not edible dry-matter yield, calorie yield, protein yield, or a complete nutritional assessment. One small aquatic crop test does not establish suitability for a diverse human diet.

- **The authors characterized microbial communities during digestion.**
  - **Evidence:** Bacterial and archaeal communities were tracked to assess which taxa contributed to digestion performance. [1]
  - **Conditions / limits:** Community characterization in one experiment does not establish operational control of a multi-year bioreactor, resistance to contamination, or restart behaviour after failure.

## Constraints and uncertainties

- The paper describes a component process. It does not close the carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, water, gas, micronutrient, or energy budgets of an integrated habitat.
- Cyanobacterial biomass production itself needs controlled light, water, gas exchange, nutrients, thermal management, harvesting, and contamination control; those upstream costs are outside this digestate result.
- Autoclaving is an energy-intensive pre-treatment. The study identifies its process benefit but does not show that it is the best system-level choice under constrained surface power and heat-recovery conditions.
- Anaerobic digestion generates biologically active material and methane. A crewed application would require gas safety, pathogen and toxin controls, crop-food-safety validation, monitoring, and rejected-batch handling.
- MGS-1 represents selected physical and chemical properties of basaltic Martian regolith. Site-specific salts, perchlorates, dust, trace metals, particle properties, and planetary-protection constraints remain unresolved.
- The study was performed under terrestrial laboratory gravity and environmental control; no partial-gravity or Mars-surface demonstration is reported.

## Operational implications

- **Established inference:** Turning biomass into fertilizer is a staged bioprocess, not direct waste-to-food conversion. Pre-treatment, temperature control, digestion, solids handling, nutrient assays, gas handling, and crop validation remain distinct operations.
- **Plausible extrapolation:** A robust habitat could couple cyanobacterial production, anaerobic digestion, hydroponics, and water recovery, but would still need imported or locally processed micronutrient supplements and independent chemical-quality checks.
- **Detail to avoid or qualify:** Do not claim that Martian regolith alone supplied a complete fertilizer, that the digestate matched standard hydroponic medium, or that the experiment demonstrated a closed-loop food system.

## Habitat Sol canon implications

- This component demonstration does not require a canon change. It supports biological nutrient recovery as one possible subsystem while preserving supplementation, monitoring, energy, food-safety, and failure-buffer requirements.
