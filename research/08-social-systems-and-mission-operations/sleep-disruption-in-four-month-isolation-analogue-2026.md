# Sleep Disruption in a Four-Month Space Analogue

**Evidence label:** Established evidence from a small controlled isolation analogue; Mars-duration and population-wide effects remain uncertain
**Last checked:** 2026-10-07
**Author:** Habitat Sol research scan
**Scope:** What repeated sleep recordings during the four-month SIRIUS-19 isolation mission show about undisturbed sleep, imposed interruptions, total sleep deprivation and portable physiological monitoring.

## Short answer

Six SIRIUS-19 crew members maintained broadly stable sleep and nocturnal autonomic measures on undisturbed nights during four months of controlled isolation. Two experimentally interrupted nights reduced sleep continuity, while one night of complete sleep deprivation produced the clearest autonomic activation. The study supports protecting uninterrupted sleep and shows that self-applied monitoring can work in confinement, but its small selected crew and single night per disturbance condition do not establish long-term Mars outcomes.

## What we know

- **The study compared several sleep conditions within the same six people.**
  - **Evidence:** The crew—three women and three men, mean age 34.3 ± 5.7 years—completed eight monitored nights: one before isolation, six during the 120-day isolation and one after. Isolation nights included three undisturbed nights, one forced all-night wake period framed as cargo unloading, two brief awakenings on another night, and one one-hour awakening on a further night. A minimum one-week washout followed impaired-sleep nights. [1]
  - **Conditions / limits:** SIRIUS-19 reproduced confinement, controlled schedules and mission-like work, not reduced gravity, Mars radiation, Martian day length or a multi-year settlement.

- **Experimentally interrupted nights reduced measured sleep continuity.**
  - **Evidence:** Mean total sleep time exceeded seven hours on undisturbed isolation days 5 and 71. Sleep efficiency averaged 77.4% on the two-awakening night and 76.8% on the one-hour-awakening night, compared with 91.9% and 92.2% on those two undisturbed nights. Total sleep time differed across study nights (repeated-measures ANOVA, p = 0.003). [1]
  - **Conditions / limits:** Sleep efficiency itself did not differ significantly across all nights (p = 0.125), and the disturbance protocols were each tested only once at fixed points in the mission.

- **Complete sleep deprivation produced the clearest measured autonomic response.**
  - **Evidence:** On the forced wake night, mean pulse rate was 66.4 ± 6.7 beats/min and mean heart-rate-variability LF/HF ratio was 2.3 ± 0.7. Across nights, pulse rate (p = 0.0001), RR interval (p = 0.004) and LF/HF (p = 0.005) differed; pulse rate and LF/HF were significantly elevated on the deprivation night compared with the other nights (p = 0.001). The authors interpret this pattern as reduced parasympathetic modulation and incomplete nocturnal autonomic recovery. [1]
  - **Conditions / limits:** LF/HF is sensitive to breathing patterns, especially during fragmented sleep, and should not be treated as a complete measure of stress or health. Daytime performance was not measured.

- **Portable self-applied monitoring was feasible in this analogue.**
  - **Evidence:** Crew members placed a medical-grade recorder's EEG, EOG, ECG and finger-plethysmography sensors themselves. The study obtained usable recordings across all conditions over the four-month campaign. [1]
  - **Conditions / limits:** The device was not formally validated against a laboratory reference during the mission. The experienced, highly selected crew may have been unusually capable of applying sensors and tolerating confinement.

## Constraints and uncertainties

- Six participants provide low statistical power and limited generalisability. Several had prior analogue or isolation experience, and all were healthy, trained volunteers selected for the mission.
- Disturbance conditions were not randomised and occurred only once each, so adaptation, mission phase and cumulative effects cannot be separated confidently from the imposed sleep condition.
- The study did not measure next-day cognitive or operational performance, clinical outcomes or repeated emergency interruptions.
- Stability on several undisturbed nights does not prove that prolonged isolation is harmless; it only describes the measured nights and variables in this crew.

## Operational implications

- **Established inference:** Planned cargo handling, alarms and maintenance that remove an entire sleep period can have a stronger immediate autonomic effect than an isolated interruption; emergency staffing and recovery time should be treated as health and operations issues.
- **Established inference:** Confinement alone did not prevent this crew from obtaining more than seven hours of measured sleep on selected undisturbed nights, so chronic sleep loss should not be assumed to be an unavoidable property of every isolated mission.
- **Plausible extrapolation:** A long-duration habitat could use portable sleep monitoring selectively after incidents or schedule changes, but thresholds for intervention would need larger longitudinal evidence and should not rely on one HRV metric.
- **Detail to avoid or qualify:** Do not generalise the apparent resilience of six experienced volunteers to children, older residents, clinical populations, shift workers or a permanent settlement.
