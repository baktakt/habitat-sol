# Post-Transit Cardiac Function Under Simulated Mars Gravity

**Evidence label:** Established observations in a small astronaut cohort under current ISS exercise countermeasures; actual post-transit performance in Martian gravity remains uncertain
**Last checked:** 2026-10-23
**Author:** Habitat Sol research scan
**Scope:** What a 2026 longitudinal study establishes about cardiac adaptation during approximately six months of microgravity and cardiovascular loading immediately after return under a 0.38 g-equivalent body tilt.

## Short answer

In 13 astronauts who spent a median of 164 days in space while using current exercise countermeasures, early reductions in left-ventricular filling and stroke volume moved back toward preflight supine values later in flight. After landing, a 22-degree head-up tilt used to impose a 0.38 g longitudinal load produced no greater measured cardiac load or hemodynamic stress than the astronauts' preflight upright posture. This is encouraging for one component of Mars arrival, but it is not direct exposure to Martian gravity and does not establish whole-body readiness, long-term health at 0.38 g, or safety after a longer transit.

## What we know

- **Cardiac changes early in the flights were measurable but did not continue worsening across the approximately six-month observation period.**
  - **Evidence:** Appadurai et al. prospectively followed 13 astronauts (nine male; mean age 49 ± 4 years) across ISS missions with a median duration of 164 days (interquartile range 126–169 days). At flight day 14, left-ventricular end-diastolic volume, stroke volume, and myocardial-deformation measures were reduced from preflight supine values. Left-ventricular end-diastolic volume and stroke volume subsequently increased toward preflight supine values during flight. [1]
  - **Conditions / limits:** This was an exploratory observational study with a small, selected astronaut cohort. The result applies to the studied cardiac measures, mission durations, and exercise-countermeasure context; it does not show that every cardiovascular or other physiological risk resolved.

- **The study tested a 0.38 g-equivalent longitudinal cardiovascular load after landing.**
  - **Evidence:** Before and after flight, echocardiography was performed during supine rest and specified upright tilts. A 22-degree head-up tilt supplied a 0.38 g component along the body axis as a simulation of Martian gravitational loading. After approximately six months in space, no measured volumetric or hemodynamic variable under this condition showed greater load or hemodynamic stress than upright posture on Earth before flight. [1]
  - **Conditions / limits:** Tilting a person in Earth's gravity reproduces a selected head-to-foot hydrostatic component; it does not reproduce a three-dimensional 0.38 g environment, locomotion, suit work, balance demands, heat load, or an actual landing on Mars.

- **The favorable result is conditional on substantial in-flight countermeasures.**
  - **Evidence:** The authors conclude that, given current countermeasures, up to six months of prolonged spaceflight did not produce large cardiac structural or functional changes beyond normal Earth-posture variation in this cohort, and that postflight cardiac response suggested orthostatic intolerance on Mars may be unlikely after such a transit. [1]
  - **Conditions / limits:** “May be unlikely” is the authors' cautious interpretation, not a measured Mars clinical-event rate. The study did not compare exercised and non-exercised groups and therefore does not isolate the countermeasures' causal effect or define a minimum effective exercise prescription.

## Constraints and uncertainties

- Thirteen participants are too few to characterize uncommon adverse outcomes, sex- or age-specific responses, medically diverse crews, or wide individual variability.
- The postflight 22-degree tilt is a cardiovascular analog, not true partial gravity. It cannot establish ability to stand, walk, lift, perform an EVA, tolerate a suit, or respond to an emergency after landing.
- The cohort's median transit analog was 164 days. Longer or more disrupted Mars transfers, reduced exercise availability, illness, injury, radiation exposure, medication, dehydration, and high landing-day workload could change the result.
- This study addresses cardiac structure, function, and selected hemodynamic measures. It does not overturn evidence gaps for bone, muscle, sensorimotor control, immunity, vision, reproduction, development, or multi-year residence at 0.38 g.

## Operational implications

- **Established inference:** A roughly six-month microgravity transit does not necessarily imply severe cardiac incapacity at Mars arrival when a crew completes current intensive exercise countermeasures.
- **Plausible extrapolation:** Arrival plans would still reasonably stage exertion, hydration, monitoring, and task complexity because the study did not test integrated surface operations or a physically demanding emergency immediately after landing.
- **Detail to avoid or qualify:** Do not describe the 22-degree tilt as direct Mars-gravity exposure or claim that this study proves astronauts will be fully fit on arrival or healthy during long residence.

## Habitat Sol canon implications

- No canon change is required. The result narrows one transit-arrival concern while leaving the library's broader conclusion intact: long-duration human health at 0.38 g has not been demonstrated.
- Any setting assumption about routine interplanetary arrival should preserve exercise, medical monitoring, and graded operational readiness rather than treating Mars gravity itself as a proven universal countermeasure.
