# Integrated Suit–Habitat CO₂ and Water Recovery Concept

**Evidence label:** Established laboratory and NASA design-study evidence; Mars operational use remains plausible extrapolation
**Last checked:** 2026-10-07
**Author:** Habitat Sol research scan
**Scope:** What the 2026 NASA–Honeywell CDRILS-I work establishes about coupling suit carbon-dioxide and humidity capture to habitat regeneration, and what remains unproven.

## Short answer

A NASA–Honeywell concept uses a circulating ionic-liquid sorbent to retain a crewmember's metabolic CO₂ and humidity in a Mars suit, then connects that loaded liquid to habitat equipment after EVA for regeneration and recovery. Subscale tests establish sorbent capacity and cyclic operation, while earlier vehicle-scale development supplies durability evidence; they do not yet demonstrate a full-size Mars suit, simultaneous suit-fluid regeneration and cabin scrubbing, or flight operation.

## What we know

- **The proposed architecture couples suit and habitat life-support functions rather than treating each as an independent consumable system.**
  - **Evidence:** Carbon Dioxide Removal by Ionic Liquid System–Integrated (CDRILS-I) combines a mobility unit (CDRILS-M) in the suit with a habitat regenerator (CDRILS-R). During EVA, a liquid pump circulates sorbent through a hollow-fibre membrane contactor to absorb CO₂ and humidity. After EVA, CDRILS-R is intended to desorb the captured materials, regenerate the sorbent and separate water from a CO₂ product stream. The design target assumes two people in a small surface habitat, both using CDRILS-M suits. [1]
  - **Conditions / limits:** This is a NASA-funded concept and development programme. The integrated two-person system has not been built or operated on Mars.

- **Subscale simulated-EVA tests found that sorbent formulation strongly controls useful capacity.**
  - **Evidence:** Tests supplied 6.5 torr CO₂ partial pressure and 30% relative humidity at a nominal simulated metabolic load of 1,200 BTU/h. The baseline ionic liquid held about 2.7% CO₂ by mass and removed 96% of supplied humidity; increasing the promoter concentration raised CO₂ capacity to about 4.1% by mass. An alternative formulation reached 7.3% by mass and ran for 12.5 hours before CO₂ breakthrough. [1]
  - **Conditions / limits:** These were subscale, ambient-pressure breadboard tests matched to selected CO₂ and water-vapour partial pressures. The authors explicitly state that the 12.5-hour test duration does not equal a qualified EVA duration; full-scale flow, suit pressure, packaging, heat rejection, toxicity and failure behaviour remain to be tested.

- **The underlying vehicle technology has meaningful ground-test history, but durability is not fully closed.**
  - **Evidence:** The programme reports more than 2.5 years of accumulated operation across active systems and more than 4.5 years including aging studies. A six-month test found no CO₂-removal-rate degradation in the ionic liquid or membranes, while longer work identified promoter degradation and membrane aging. Stabilisation and coating changes are expected—not yet proven—to support a three-year maintenance interval. [1]
  - **Conditions / limits:** Accumulated programme runtime is distributed across several demonstrators and aging tests; it is not one integrated suit–habitat unit operating continuously for years.

- **The habitat regenerator has a demanding simultaneous duty.**
  - **Evidence:** The planned CDRILS-R must regenerate loaded suit sorbent while continuing to remove cabin CO₂. The concept also permits downstream Sabatier processing of recovered CO₂ with hydrogen to make methane and water. [1]
  - **Conditions / limits:** The paper does not provide a demonstrated simultaneous-duty performance margin, complete energy balance, full water-recovery efficiency, leak inventory, spare-parts burden or emergency mode. Sabatier and possible methane pyrolysis add separate hardware and operating constraints.

## Constraints and uncertainties

- A circulating liquid inside a wearable life-support loop introduces pumps, seals, contactors, heat exchange and sorbent containment whose integrated reliability and maintainability are not established by the subscale tests.
- The alternative sorbent with the highest reported capacity is not identified chemically in the paper; its toxicity, long-term stability and compatibility require qualification.
- The authors plan full-scale PLSS-pressure testing, system requirements work in 2027 and a possible technology demonstration in 2028 or 2029. These are programme goals, not achieved milestones.
- CDRILS-I may reduce vented CO₂ and water and share hardware knowledge between suits and habitats, but the paper does not prove lower total system mass, power, logistics or risk than competing Mars PLSS architectures.

## Operational implications

- **Established inference:** A regenerable suit can shift EVA consumables into a post-EVA service workload; captured material still has to be transferred, regenerated, separated, monitored and stored.
- **Established inference:** Suit availability becomes coupled to habitat regenerator capacity. If multiple loaded sorbent inventories return together, the habitat must maintain cabin scrubbing while processing them.
- **Plausible extrapolation:** A mature implementation would likely track each sorbent batch's loading, contamination and aging, with swap-ready liquid modules and independent emergency CO₂ removal if pumps or the central regenerator are unavailable.
- **Detail to avoid or qualify:** Do not equate the 12.5-hour subscale run with a 12.5-hour EVA rating, or describe laboratory capture as proof of complete water and oxygen loop closure.
