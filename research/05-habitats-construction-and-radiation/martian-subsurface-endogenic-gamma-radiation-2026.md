# Endogenic Gamma Radiation in the Martian Subsurface

**Evidence label:** Established orbital-composition data interpreted through a peer-reviewed dosimetry model; local subsurface dose and total human exposure remain uncertain
**Last checked:** 2026-10-09
**Author:** Habitat Sol research scan
**Scope:** What a 2026 global model establishes about gamma radiation produced by potassium, thorium and uranium in the Martian crust, distinct from cosmic-ray and solar-particle exposure.

## Short answer

A model based on Mars Odyssey gamma-ray measurements estimates that crustal potassium, thorium and inferred uranium produce an average gamma absorbed-dose rate of 7.04 ± 0.15 nGy/h at the surface and 10 m depth, equivalent to about 0.12 mGy/year, with mapped values of 4.5–10.5 nGy/h. This endogenic component is much smaller than measured unshielded surface radiation from space, but it is spatially variable and becomes the residual background once overburden suppresses exogenic particles. The model is regional and does not constitute a site survey, a total habitat dose, or a human effective-dose assessment.

## What we know

- **Natural radioactivity in Martian rock creates a low but non-zero subsurface gamma field.**
  - **Evidence:** Gary-Bicas et al. combined Mars Odyssey Gamma Ray Spectrometer maps of potassium and thorium with an inferred uranium abundance and terrestrial dosimetry relationships. Their global mean gamma absorbed-dose rate is 7.04 ± 0.15 nGy/h, with mapped extrema of 4.5–10.5 nGy/h; the reported mean annual dose at the surface and 10 m depth is about 0.12 mGy/year. [1]
  - **Conditions / limits:** The unit is absorbed dose in grays, not equivalent or effective dose in sieverts. Organ dose and health risk depend on radiation type, geometry, occupancy and biological weighting.

- **The model predicts lateral variation tied to crustal chemistry.**
  - **Evidence:** Higher modelled rates occur in parts of the northern lowlands, including Acidalia, Chryse, Utopia and Isidis Planitia, and in parts of Terra Cimmeria and Terra Sirenum. At 10 m depth the full mapped annual range is approximately 0.03–0.17 mGy/year. [1]
  - **Conditions / limits:** Input elemental maps have 5° × 5° resolution and sample roughly the upper metre. These regional cells cannot resolve a specific cave, excavation face, imported aggregate, ore body or building material.

- **The crustal component is small relative to the present unshielded surface radiation environment.**
  - **Evidence:** The paper contrasts its approximately 0.12 mGy/year endogenic global mean with about 76 mGy/year average absorbed dose measured at the surface by Curiosity's Radiation Assessment Detector, where galactic cosmic rays and solar energetic particles dominate. [1][2]
  - **Conditions / limits:** The two figures describe different radiation fields and geometries. They should not be subtracted mechanically to design shielding or converted one-for-one into human effective dose.

- **Ten metres of depth does not materially increase the modelled endogenic field.**
  - **Evidence:** Although the study assumes heat-producing-element abundance rises exponentially toward the base of the crust, it finds negligible difference between surface and 10 m endogenic gamma estimates because 10 m is shallow relative to crustal thickness. Much larger depth-dependent changes appear only across the middle and bottom of the crust. [1]
  - **Conditions / limits:** This finding concerns radiation generated by the crust. The protective effect of overburden against cosmic rays and solar particles comes from earlier transport models cited by the authors, not from their endogenic gamma calculation.

## Constraints and uncertainties

- Uranium was not mapped directly. The study inferred it from thorium using a cosmochemical Th/U ratio and explored alternative ratios; local uranium enrichment could depart from that assumption.
- The authors used terrestrial activity-to-dose equations. They state that Mars-calibrated relationships would improve precision.
- Gamma-ray spectrometer abundance uncertainties were propagated, but unresolved local geology, density, voids, construction materials and radon or other decay-product transport are outside this regional model.
- The paper's statement that the 10 m environment is safe for humans should be read narrowly: its new calculation covers endogenic gamma absorbed dose. A complete underground-habitat assessment must also model residual GCR/SEP secondaries, geometry, material composition, occupancy and agency exposure limits.
- The study also estimates microbial destruction times using *Deinococcus radiodurans*. Those calculations are relevant to biosignature preservation and planetary protection, not a direct measure of human health or habitat cleanliness.

## Operational implications

- **Established inference:** Once overburden removes most external particle radiation, naturally radioactive rock remains a small site-dependent background rather than falling to zero.
- **Established inference:** Regional orbital maps are useful for screening, but a final subsurface site or regolith-derived building material requires local gamma spectroscopy and dosimetry.
- **Plausible extrapolation:** A mature buried habitat could include radiological characterization in excavation surveys and acceptance testing for regolith-based blocks, especially where local material is concentrated or moved indoors.
- **Detail to avoid or qualify:** Do not quote 0.12 mGy/year as total underground crew exposure, describe 10 m as a universally sufficient shielding depth, or treat absorbed dose in mGy as interchangeable with effective dose in mSv.

## Habitat Sol canon implications

- No canon change is required. The result supports underground shielding against exogenic radiation while preserving a low, measurable natural gamma background and a need for local material surveys.
- A canon decision about habitat depth or radiation safety still requires a complete shielding geometry and total-exposure budget; this paper alone does not supply either.
