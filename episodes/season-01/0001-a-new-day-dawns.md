# A New Day Dawns

The darkness outside the kitchen window had a weight to it before dawn. Then, almost without drama, it began to loosen.

Mira watched the far side of the dust field turn the color of old brick. The window gave it back in a dull reflection: her mug, the tool pouch Kenji had forgotten on the counter, Tomas's school gloves drying on the radiator. In the glass, the sun looked too small to have made the change. Outside the habitat, nothing had become kinder. Inside, the tea tasted stronger for the sealed warmth of the room.

She had lived long enough on Mars to know that the sunrise was not a promise. Still, she waited for it every morning she could.

Lina's pod door was shut. A thin line of light showed beneath it; she had probably fallen asleep with a stream playing again. Tomas had managed to put one sock on before abandoning the effort, and now the other lay under the table with a small brass washer beside it. Mira moved the washer onto the tool pouch without thinking. Kenji would be looking for it later. Tomas would notice it was gone.

The bathroom fan stopped. Kenji came out rubbing a towel over his close-cropped hair, already in orange work trousers and a thermal shirt. He carried his gloves by two fingers, as if they had personally offended him.

“Morning,” Mira said.

“Is it?” He bent over the counter, looking for the pouch.

“Dawn, at least.”

“That is more defensible.” He found the pouch, opened it, and immediately looked at her. “You reorganized my disorder.”

“I returned a washer to its natural habitat.”

He smiled, but it came late. The three dust-harvesting rovers had been taking turns becoming impossible for a week. One fault had fed into the next: a grit-scored coupling, an arm that would not hold calibration, a sensor that reported clear air while coughing dust through its housing. Kenji had been leaving after dinner and returning before breakfast with the dust still caught in the folds of his workwear.

He set the kettle to heat again. “Last one tonight,” he said. “I think.”

Mira looked at him over the rim of her mug.

“I said I think.”

“And then?”

“Then tomorrow we have dinner together. The whole family. I will make something incapable of being eaten from a packet.”

“That sounds ambitious.”

“I repair harvesters. I can manage noodles.”

From behind Lina's door came a muffled sound of disapproval, followed by the quick tap of a message being sent. Kenji glanced toward it and lowered his voice.

“No reservations?”

Mira wanted to say that the rovers might not be done, that the greenhouse figures on her tablet might not hold through another review, that every plan in Sol had a small red mark beside it waiting to become urgent. Instead she said, “No reservations.”

He nodded as if that settled a contract between them.

At the table, Mira opened the greenhouse report. The modified plants had been an argument for months: altered feed ratios, different light cycles, a stubborn line of leafy greens that seemed to need less persuasion than the tomatoes. The new nutrition readings were not miraculous. They were a handful of better columns, a slight lift where the numbers had been stubbornly flat.

But they were real.

She ran the values once more, then again with yesterday's calibration notes beside them. The numbers stayed where they were.

Kenji slid a fresh cup of tea toward her. “Good?”

“Better than good,” she said, and heard how quickly she had said it.

He did not ask her to qualify it. That was one of the ways they had remained married: he knew when a question would turn a small piece of hope into a report.

Tomas appeared in the doorway, dressed now, his hair standing up on one side. He looked first at the sunrise, then at the tool pouch.

“That's mine,” he said.

“The washer?” Kenji asked.

“It was thinking.”

“Was it.”

“It was in the corridor and it was thinking about the rover.” Tomas climbed onto his chair. “I brought it home so it wouldn't be lonely.”

Kenji considered this with the seriousness he gave faulty seals. “Then it has had an important night.”

Lina emerged behind him, hood up, tablet under one arm. She did not look at the window. “Are you working late again?” she asked Kenji.

“Just tonight. Then dinner tomorrow.”

“Sure.” She poured herself tea and stood rather than sitting.

Mira saw the bruise of tiredness beneath her daughter's eyes and the bright, guarded impatience that made every reassurance sound like a lie. She wanted to tell Lina about the readings—not to make her stay, only to let the house have one good piece of news in it. But Lina had heard too many adults turn fragile systems into reasons she should be grateful.

So Mira closed the report.

“School,” she said gently.

Lina looked at her, understood the avoidance exactly, and lifted one shoulder. “Right.”

The greenhouse complex smelled of wet substrate and warm metal when Mira arrived. Condensation had gathered along the inner panels, turning the first red light outside into a blurry stain. Someone had left a pruning cart crooked in the aisle. A strip of old tape held one of its wheels together; the tape was a shade too bright for the cart, a repair made in a hurry and kept because it worked.

Mira straightened it before she reached the monitoring station.

The young plants waited under their lamps, ordinary and green. Their leaves did not announce themselves. The irrigation lines clicked softly overhead. Beyond the glass, Mars kept brightening, compact darkness giving way to rust.

Mira placed her palm against the sensor housing and pulled up the report again.

There it was: not safety, not an answer, but a direction.

For the next meeting she would say, We are making progress. The sentence was true, though it would not calm everyone on the agronomy board, and it would not make tomorrow's dinner happen. It would not tell her whether Sol could keep arranging its life around narrow margins forever.

But she could bring the numbers to her team. She could ask them to check them. She could let the work belong to more than one pair of hands.

Outside, the sun was fully up. Inside the greenhouse, Mira began the day.
